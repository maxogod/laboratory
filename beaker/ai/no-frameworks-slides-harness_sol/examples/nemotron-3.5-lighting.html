The Value of Offline Games
The current landscape

Single-player games that need the internet

Many modern single-player titles require an online connection at launch — not for multiplayer, but for license key checking, DRM validation, or account authentication.

  • Always-online DRM: The game phones home to verify your purchase before letting you play.
  • Launcher dependency: You must log into a store/client before the game will start.
  • Day-one patches: Some discs or downloads are incomplete without an internet update.
  • Single-player modes gated online: Campaigns that disconnect you if your network drops.

Bottom line: You bought a solo experience, but it still needs permission from a server to run.

The Value of Offline Games
Definition

What makes a game "offline"?

  • No login required. Install and play without an account.
  • No launch-time key check. The game doesn't phone home before the title screen.
  • No disconnect penalties. Losing Wi-Fi mid-session doesn't kick you out.
  • Fully playable years later. Even if the original store or publisher shuts down.

Offline

Works on a plane, in a cabin, during an outage, or a decade after release.

Online-required

Stops working when servers go down, your internet drops, or your account is banned.

The Value of Offline Games
Game history is fragile

Offline games are preservable

When a game's only copy depends on an external authentication server, its lifespan is tied to that server's uptime — not to the game itself.

  • Server sunsets: Publishers retire authentication servers; online-required solo games become unlaunchable.
  • Store closures: Digital storefronts and launchers disappear, taking your "library" with them.
  • No archival: Libraries and museums cannot preserve a game that won't run without a live license check.

Offline = immortal

A DRM-free installer or cartridge can be backed up, emulated, and played indefinitely — independent of any company's server.

Who gets to play?

Internet is not universal

  • Rural and remote areas often lack reliable broadband.
  • Data caps and metered connections make large daily checks impractical.
  • Travelers, military personnel, and students may not have consistent access.
  • Developing regions face cost and infrastructure barriers.

You bought it. Or did you?

Ownership vs. license

An online key check transforms a purchase into a conditional lease. You don't own the game — you hold permission that can be revoked.

  • Account bans can lock you out of single-player games you paid for.
  • Payment disputes or region changes can suspend your library.
  • Terms of service changes can alter how — or whether — your game runs.
  • Offline installers mean actual possession: the game is yours, on your terms.
The Value of Offline Games
For creators

Design without servers

  • Focus on the game, not the netcode. Resources go into story, mechanics, and polish — not authentication APIs.
  • No launch-day server meltdowns. Players can start playing the moment they install.
  • Lower long-term costs. No authentication infrastructure to maintain for years after release.
  • Trust the player. DRM-free releases build goodwill and reduce friction between creator and audience.

Great single-player design doesn't need a handshake with a remote server.

The Value of Offline Games
Immersion, uninterrupted

Solo play should be solitary

  • No connection pop-ups breaking narrative immersion.
  • No ping spikes stuttering a cutscene or boss fight.
  • No forced updates blocking you right when you want to play.
  • Quiet, personal, on-demand. The game waits for you, not the other way around.

"The best single-player games feel like a conversation between the player and the world — not between the player and a server."

Real-world examples

When online requirements fail

SimCity (2013)

Required a constant connection for its single-player city simulation. Launch-day server failures left paying customers unable to play at all.

Elden Ring / Witcher 3 (offline)

Massive, critically acclaimed single-player experiences fully playable without an internet connection — proving AAA scale and offline play coexist.

Diablo III at launch

Online requirement caused Error 37 login queues for players who only wanted to play solo.

GOG classics

DRM-free installers mean games from decades ago still install and run today.

Alternatives exist

The DRM-free and offline movement

  • GOG.com: Built on the principle that games you buy should be yours, DRM-free and offline.
  • itch.io: Many indie developers sell direct, offline installers with no launcher required.
  • Humble Bundle: Often provides DRM-free downloads alongside Steam keys.
  • Physical media: Cartridges and discs that work without day-one patches or online checks.

Supporting offline releases is a vote for games that outlive their publishers.

Take action

What you can do

  • Check before you buy. Look for "offline," "DRM-free," or "single-player" labels on store pages.
  • Back up your installers. Keep local copies of games you own so they survive store closures.
  • Support DRM-free storefronts. Your money signals that offline access matters.
  • Speak up. Review-bombing isn't the answer — clear, honest feedback about always-online requirements is.

Every purchase is a vote for the kind of games industry we want.
